R01 · Reconstruction & rights · 1865–1868

Who would rebuild the Union?

Andrew Johnson favored rapid restoration of former Confederate states. Congressional Republicans demanded stronger protection for freedpeople. The Reconstruction Acts placed most of the former Confederacy under military districts and established conditions for readmission. Johnson’s conflict with Congress culminated in impeachment; the Senate acquitted him in 1868.

Why it matters: Reconstruction was a struggle over who would define freedom and who had authority to enforce it.

Know these: Andrew Johnson; Thaddeus Stevens; Charles Sumner; Reconstruction Acts; impeachment.

Think it through: Why might quick reunification conflict with protecting newly freed people?

R02 · Reconstruction & rights · 1865 / 1868 / 1870

Three amendments, three different jobs

The 13th Amendment abolished slavery, with an exception for punishment after criminal conviction. The 14th established national citizenship and protections including due process and equal protection. The 15th prohibited voting discrimination based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

Why it matters: Constitutional rights still depended on enforcement. The 15th did not establish universal suffrage or eliminate every device used to suppress voting.

Know these: 13th: slavery; 14th: citizenship and equal protection; 15th: racial voting discrimination.

Think it through: How can a constitutional right exist while many people remain unable to exercise it?

R03 · Reconstruction & rights · 1865–1901

Freedom, land, and dependence

Freedpeople built families, schools, churches, and political organizations. But most lacked land and capital. Sharecropping exchanged labor for a share of a crop; crop-lien credit pledged future harvests. Black Codes and exploitative labor practices constrained bargaining power.

Why it matters: Legal emancipation did not automatically produce economic independence. Track ownership, access to credit, and control over labor separately.

Know these: Freedmen’s Bureau; Special Field Order No. 15; sharecropping; crop lien; convict leasing.

Think it through: Why could a farmer work all year and still finish the year in debt?

R04 · Reconstruction & rights · 1866–1876

Violence and the struggle to enforce rights

White supremacist groups attacked Black voters, officeholders, teachers, and white Republicans. General Reynolds’s Texas report describes intimidation and weak local protection. Congress’s Enforcement Acts expanded federal tools against interference with voting and civil rights.

Why it matters: Political violence could change election outcomes without changing constitutional language. Compare what federal officials authorized with what they could actually enforce.

Know these: Ku Klux Klan; Ulysses S. Grant; Enforcement Acts; Colfax Massacre; Reynolds.

Think it through: How did violence help opponents of Reconstruction regain political control?

R05 · Reconstruction & rights · 1876–1877

A disputed election and Reconstruction’s retreat

Samuel Tilden led the popular vote, but disputed electoral returns left the presidency unresolved. An electoral commission awarded the disputed votes to Rutherford B. Hayes, producing a 185–184 result. In 1877 Hayes withdrew troops protecting the remaining Republican state governments in Louisiana and South Carolina.

Why it matters: The political settlement accelerated Reconstruction’s collapse. Avoid claiming that every federal soldier left the South or that all Black political participation ended immediately.

Know these: Rutherford B. Hayes; Samuel Tilden; Electoral Commission; Compromise of 1877.

Think it through: Why did a presidential election dispute have consequences for civil rights in the South?

R06 · Reconstruction & rights · 1877–1901

Jim Crow and resistance

Disfranchisement, segregation, lynching, and economic coercion narrowed Black freedom. Plessy upheld racial segregation in railway cars in 1896. Ida B. Wells investigated lynching and challenged its stated justifications. Booker T. Washington and W. E. B. Du Bois offered differing strategies for advancement.

Why it matters: Black Americans resisted through journalism, education, organizing, migration, and litigation. Do not reduce their experiences to passive victimhood.

Know these: Ida B. Wells; Booker T. Washington; W. E. B. Du Bois; grandfather clause; Wilmington coup.

Think it through: How did legal restrictions and extralegal violence reinforce each other?

E01 · Industry & economics · 1862–1901

Railroads build a national economy

The Pacific Railway Acts supported transcontinental construction with federal assistance. The Central Pacific and Union Pacific joined in Utah in 1869. Railroads connected producers and buyers, expanded demand for steel and coal, and made rates, rebates, and access to markets central political issues.

Why it matters: Large private enterprises grew partly through public land and financing. Ask who gained access to markets and who bore the costs of expansion.

Know these: Central Pacific; Union Pacific; Leland Stanford; Cornelius Vanderbilt; rebates; time zones.

Think it through: How could railroads both lower transportation costs and exploit dependent customers?

E02 · Industry & economics · 1870s–1901

Steel, oil, and business combinations

Andrew Carnegie became associated with steel and vertical integration: control over successive stages of production. John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil through consolidation, including horizontal integration: combining competitors at the same stage. Trusts and holding companies coordinated control over multiple businesses.

Why it matters: Scale could reduce costs while concentration limited competition. A firm could use more than one method of integration.

Know these: Carnegie; Rockefeller; Henry Clay Frick; Standard Oil; Bessemer process; trust.

Think it through: Compare owning mines and mills with buying rival mills. What problem does each strategy solve?

E03 · Industry & economics · 1869–1901

Finance, speculation, and consolidation

Railroads and factories required large pools of investment. Bankers such as J. P. Morgan reorganized firms and helped consolidate industries; U.S. Steel formed in 1901. Speculators could manipulate markets: Jay Gould and James Fisk’s attempt to corner gold ended in Black Friday in 1869.

Why it matters: Distinguish raising productive capital from speculative gains. Ownership of shares and control of a company are related but different.

Know these: J. P. Morgan; Jay Gould; James Fisk; stocks; bonds; U.S. Steel.

Think it through: Why might investors favor a merger even when workers or consumers oppose it?

E04 · Industry & economics · 1860s–1901

Invention changes daily life and work

Telephone networks, electric lighting, improved steel production, and urban transit changed communication, factories, and cities. Alexander Graham Bell, Thomas Edison, Lewis Latimer, and George Westinghouse belong in a network of invention, patents, investment, and skilled labor.

Why it matters: An invention matters historically when institutions make it widely usable. Connect technology to new jobs, work rhythms, and unequal access.

Know these: Bell; Edison; Latimer; Westinghouse; telephone; phonograph; electricity.

Think it through: Why is an inventor’s patent only one part of the story of technological change?

E05 · Industry & economics · 1873 / 1893

Two panics, widespread hardship

The Panic of 1873 followed financial instability and railroad overinvestment; Jay Cooke & Company’s failure became a major trigger. In 1893, railroad and bank failures again spread distress. Depositors demanded cash, lending contracted, firms failed, and workers faced unemployment or wage cuts.

Why it matters: A financial shock spreads into everyday life through credit, business investment, employment, and public confidence. The two panics are separate assigned events.

Know these: Jay Cooke; bank run; credit contraction; depression; railroad securities.

Think it through: Trace a chain from a bank failure to a strike or political protest.

E06 · Industry & economics · 1873–1900

Gold, silver, and the burden of debt

Free-silver advocates wanted expanded silver coinage, hoping to increase the money supply and raise prices. Gold-standard supporters favored a stable gold-based currency. Falling prices could increase the real burden of a fixed debt, especially for farmers whose crop income declined.

Why it matters: Money debates were arguments about who would gain or lose from changing prices. Free silver did not mean handing out free money.

Know these: Coinage Act of 1873; Bland-Allison Act; Sherman Silver Purchase Act; Gold Standard Act.

Think it through: If crop prices fall but a loan stays the same, what happens to the amount of crop needed to repay it?

L01 · Labor & conflict · 1866–1890s

Different strategies for organizing labor

The Knights of Labor sought a broad organization of workers and wide social reform, though its inclusion had limits. The American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, emphasized skilled craft unions, wages, hours, and working conditions.

Why it matters: A union’s membership and goals affect its bargaining strategy. Do not treat all unions as identical or assume that every union included all workers.

Know these: Terence Powderly; Samuel Gompers; Knights of Labor; AFL; collective bargaining.

Think it through: What advantages and limits come with organizing by craft rather than across an entire industry?

L02 · Labor & conflict · 1877

Great Railroad Strike

Railroad wage cuts during a prolonged depression sparked a strike beginning in Martinsburg, West Virginia, and spreading across major rail centers. State militias and federal troops intervened. The strike revealed both workers’ ability to disrupt national commerce and the power available to employers.

Why it matters: Know the economic context, the transportation network, and the government response—not only the date.

Know these: Baltimore & Ohio Railroad; Martinsburg; Pittsburgh; Rutherford B. Hayes.

Think it through: How did a connected railroad system help a local dispute become a national crisis?

L03 · Labor & conflict · 1886

Haymarket and the eight-hour movement

At a Chicago labor rally on May 4, a bomb was thrown as police moved to disperse the crowd. Gunfire followed. The bombing and controversial prosecution of anarchists intensified public suspicion of radicalism and labor organizing.

Why it matters: Distinguish the broader eight-hour campaign, the rally, the unidentified bomber, and the people prosecuted. They are not interchangeable.

Know these: Chicago; eight-hour day; Albert and Lucy Parsons; anarchism; John Peter Altgeld.

Think it through: How can public reaction to violence affect people who did not commit it?

L04 · Labor & conflict · 1892

Homestead: steelworkers and Pinkertons

At Carnegie’s Homestead steelworks in Pennsylvania, Henry Clay Frick’s confrontation with the union escalated into a battle between workers and Pinkerton guards. The state militia intervened, and the union suffered a major defeat.

Why it matters: Compare Carnegie’s public language about wealth and responsibility with labor relations in his industry. Philanthropy and bargaining power are different questions.

Know these: Henry Clay Frick; Andrew Carnegie; Pinkertons; Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers.

Think it through: What did control of the plant, security forces, and state support mean for the balance of power?

L05 · Labor & conflict · 1894

Pullman: wages, rents, and federal power

After wage reductions at Pullman’s company town, workers struck. Eugene Debs’s American Railway Union supported a boycott of Pullman cars. Federal authorities used an injunction and troops, citing interference with the mail and interstate commerce. The strike collapsed.

Why it matters: Compare direct bargaining with a nationwide solidarity boycott. A company town links an employer’s workplace power to workers’ housing costs.

Know these: George Pullman; Eugene Debs; American Railway Union; Grover Cleveland; injunction.

Think it through: Why did rent matter when wages fell? How did the mail become a federal issue?

S01 · Immigration & society · 1865–1901

Migration and the industrial workforce

Immigrants supplied labor and built neighborhoods, businesses, religious institutions, and mutual-aid networks. Migration patterns diversified late in the century, including more arrivals from southern and eastern Europe. Ellis Island opened in 1892; it was not the entry point for every immigrant.

Why it matters: Connect migration to both opportunity and exclusion. Working conditions, family strategies, and community institutions help explain settlement patterns.

Know these: Ellis Island; mutual aid; nativism; tenement; ethnic neighborhood.

Think it through: How could an ethnic neighborhood provide protection while also reflecting limited choices?

S02 · Immigration & society · 1875–1898

Exclusion and citizenship

Anti-Chinese activism linked racial prejudice with fears about wages and employment. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 suspended immigration of Chinese laborers and restricted naturalization. Wong Kim Ark later affirmed citizenship for a U.S.-born child of Chinese parents under the circumstances in that case.

Why it matters: Immigration admission, naturalization, and birthright citizenship are distinct legal questions. A restriction on one does not automatically resolve the others.

Know these: Page Act; Denis Kearney; Chinese Exclusion Act; Wong Kim Ark; Rock Springs Massacre.

Think it through: How could exclusionary immigration policy coexist with a ruling affirming birthright citizenship?

S03 · Immigration & society · 1880s–1901

Cities, housing, and reform

Industrial cities concentrated opportunity alongside crowded housing and unhealthy conditions. Jacob Riis used reporting and photography to publicize tenement life. Jane Addams and settlement workers built neighborhood institutions intended to connect reformers and residents through education, services, and civic participation.

Why it matters: Reformers diagnosed different causes of poverty and did not always escape the prejudices of their era. Evaluate both their evidence and their assumptions.

Know these: Jacob Riis; Jane Addams; Hull House; settlement house; tenement.

Think it through: Compare exposing a problem to living and working within a community. How might the methods produce different solutions?

S04 · Immigration & society · 1873–1901

Women’s rights and public activism

Women organized for suffrage, temperance, education, labor reform, and professional access. Bradwell illustrates a barrier to entering the legal profession. Suffrage organizations merged into NAWSA in 1890. Opportunities varied sharply by race, class, marital status, and region.

Why it matters: Political participation extends beyond casting a ballot. Organizing, testimony, journalism, and professional work could challenge gender boundaries.

Know these: Frances Willard; Victoria Woodhull; Belva Lockwood; NAWSA; WCTU; coverture.

Think it through: How did reform work create arguments for women’s citizenship and political power?

S05 · Immigration & society · 1879–1890s

Competing explanations of inequality

Carnegie defended wealth concentration while calling for responsible philanthropy. Sumner argued that interference with competition could be harmful. Henry George focused on land and proposed a single tax on land values. Reformers and labor activists offered still other explanations for poverty.

Why it matters: Learn each thinker’s diagnosis, proposed remedy, and view of government. Philanthropy, laissez-faire, and structural reform are not the same program.

Know these: Gospel of Wealth; Social Darwinism; single tax; Social Gospel; laissez-faire.

Think it through: Which thinker would change institutions, which would stress private giving, and which would resist intervention?

S06 · Immigration & society · 1870s–1901

Culture, consumption, and the White City

Department stores, mail-order catalogs, entertainment, and new media expanded consumer culture. Chicago’s World’s Columbian Exposition in 1893 showcased architecture, landscape design, and technology. Its displays also reflected hierarchies in who could represent the nation and its progress.

Why it matters: A celebration of progress is evidence of aspiration and selective presentation. Ask whose labor, hardship, and exclusion sit outside the advertised image.

Know these: World’s Columbian Exposition; Frederick Law Olmsted; vaudeville; conspicuous consumption; Mark Twain.

Think it through: How could the 1893 fair celebrate prosperity during a financial crisis?

P01 · Politics & reform · 1860s–1890s

Machines, patronage, and political power

Urban political machines exchanged practical help, jobs, and favors for loyalty. Tammany Hall became closely associated with New York politics and William “Boss” Tweed’s corruption. Patronage also shaped national party conflict.

Why it matters: Machine politics mixed real services with unequal access and misuse of public money. Explain why voters might support a machine without ignoring corruption.

Know these: Tammany Hall; William Tweed; Thomas Nast; patronage; spoils system.

Think it through: Why might a new immigrant value a machine’s help more than an abstract reform promise?

P02 · Politics & reform · 1883–1890

Reform and regulation: three federal responses

The Pendleton Act created a merit-based civil-service system for selected federal positions. The Interstate Commerce Act created the ICC to regulate railroads. The Sherman Antitrust Act targeted restraints of trade and monopolization. Each addressed a different problem.

Why it matters: The enactment of a law does not prove it immediately solved its problem. Enforcement, coverage, courts, and political commitment shaped results.

Know these: Pendleton: hiring; ICC: railroads; Sherman: competition; Wabash; John Sherman.

Think it through: What evidence would show that a reform changed behavior rather than simply changing the law?

P03 · Politics & reform · 1870s–1892

Farmers organize and Populism grows

Farmers faced debt, uncertain crop prices, transportation costs, and dependence on lenders and merchants. Alliances promoted cooperation and political reform. The People’s Party’s Omaha Platform called for changes including expanded currency, a graduated income tax, and public control of major transportation and communications systems.

Why it matters: Farm protest was about power in markets as well as prices. Shared economic grievances did not erase racial barriers to cooperation.

Know these: Grange; Farmers’ Alliances; Colored Farmers’ Alliance; Mary Lease; James B. Weaver.

Think it through: How would a cooperative store, a currency change, and railroad regulation address different parts of farm hardship?

P04 · Politics & reform · 1896–1900

The election of 1896

Democrat William Jennings Bryan made free silver central to his campaign and gained Populist backing. Republican William McKinley defended gold and protective tariffs, with Mark Hanna directing major campaign efforts. McKinley won. The Gold Standard Act followed in 1900.

Why it matters: Connect the election to conflicting ideas about economic recovery. The result did not mean that all rural voters favored Bryan or all workers favored McKinley.

Know these: Bryan; McKinley; Mark Hanna; Cross of Gold; William Allen White; protective tariff.

Think it through: How did Bryan and his critics describe the relationship between government policy and prosperity?

W01 · Expansion & the West · 1862–1890s

Land, settlement, and dispossession

The Homestead Act offered claims to eligible settlers who met its conditions. Federal land policies and railroad construction encouraged farming, ranching, mining, and migration. These policies also accelerated the loss of Indigenous land and intensified conflict over resources.

Why it matters: Land described by settlers as available was often already inhabited or used by Native peoples. Climate and access to credit limited the promise of independent farming.

Know these: Homestead Act; Morrill Act; 100th meridian; long drive; barbed wire; land rush.

Think it through: How did government policy, business investment, and environmental limits interact in western settlement?

W02 · Expansion & the West · 1869–1890s

Assimilation and the Dawes Act

Federal policy pressured Native peoples onto reservations and sought cultural assimilation. The Dawes Act of 1887 divided many communally held lands into individual allotments and opened remaining lands to non-Native acquisition. Boarding schools such as Carlisle targeted Native languages and cultural practices.

Why it matters: Assimilation was a coercive policy, not simply an expansion of educational opportunity. Allotment undermined collective landholding and tribal sovereignty.

Know these: Dawes Act; allotment; Carlisle; sovereignty; Grant’s Peace Policy; Wounded Knee.

Think it through: How can a policy framed as individual opportunity weaken a community’s control of its future?

W03 · Expansion & the West · 1890–1893

The frontier as an interpretation

After the 1890 census reported the disappearance of a continuous frontier line, Frederick Jackson Turner argued in 1893 that frontier settlement had shaped American institutions and identity. This was an influential historical thesis, not a neutral description of all western experiences.

Why it matters: Evaluate what Turner emphasizes and what his model overlooks, including Native sovereignty, women, racial conflict, federal policy, and corporate investment.

Know these: Frederick Jackson Turner; frontier thesis; 1890 census; democracy; historical interpretation.

Think it through: How would the story change if told from the perspective of a railroad laborer or a Native community?

W04 · Expansion & the West · 1867–1901

From continental expansion to overseas power

The United States purchased Alaska in 1867. In 1898 it annexed Hawaii and fought Spain; the resulting treaty transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to U.S. control. War followed against Filipino independence forces. Expansion raised disputes over citizenship, trade, race, and self-government.

Why it matters: Use overseas expansion as context within the annual topic while giving priority to the specifically assigned sources and terms.

Know these: Alaska; Hawaii; Spanish-American War; Philippines; imperialism; anti-imperialism.

Think it through: How did acquiring overseas territory expose tensions between national power and consent of the governed?

The ten assigned court cases

Learn the issue, result, and significance—not just the case name. Dates below are decision years; some legal databases display the Court’s term year in their headings.

Case / yearQuestionResult and distinction
Bradwell v. Illinois
1873

Read opinion ↗

Could a woman claim a federal constitutional right to enter the legal profession?

Connect: Women’s rights; limits of national citizenship

The Court rejected Myra Bradwell’s claim under the Privileges or Immunities Clause. Distinguish the majority’s reasoning from Justice Bradley’s separate argument about women’s roles.
Slaughterhouse Cases
1873

Read opinion ↗

Did a state slaughterhouse monopoly violate the 14th Amendment?

Connect: Limits of the Privileges or Immunities Clause

The Court upheld the monopoly and interpreted national privileges or immunities narrowly, separating national from state citizenship.
United States v. Reese
1876

Read opinion ↗

How far could federal voting-rights enforcement reach?

Connect: Voting rights; enforcement limits

The Court invalidated provisions that were not confined to racial discrimination. It treated the 15th Amendment as a ban on specified discrimination, not a general grant of suffrage.
Munn v. Illinois
1877

Read opinion ↗

Could Illinois regulate grain-storage rates?

Connect: Public interest; state regulation

The Court upheld regulation of private businesses affected with a public interest. This concerned grain warehouses, not a blanket permission to regulate interstate rail traffic.
Civil Rights Cases
1883

Read opinion ↗

Could Congress ban discrimination in private public accommodations under the Reconstruction Amendments?

Connect: State action versus private discrimination

The Court struck down key provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1875. Its 14th Amendment analysis required state action; it also rejected the asserted 13th Amendment basis.
Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad
1886

Read opinion ↗

How did a railroad tax dispute connect to corporate constitutional protection?

Connect: Corporate personhood; read the holding carefully

The case is associated with applying equal protection to corporations. The famous statement appears in the report before the opinion; the opinion resolved the assessment on narrower grounds.
Wabash v. Illinois
1886

Read opinion ↗

Could Illinois regulate rates on interstate railroad shipments?

Connect: Federal versus state commerce power

The Court limited state regulation of interstate commerce. The decision helped prompt the federal Interstate Commerce Act of 1887.
Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway v. Minnesota
1890

Read opinion ↗

Could a commission’s railroad rates be final without judicial review of reasonableness?

Connect: Rate regulation; due process; judicial review

The Court held that denying meaningful judicial review in this setting violated due process. Compare the decision’s limits with Munn’s approval of regulation.
Plessy v. Ferguson
1896

Read opinion ↗

Did Louisiana’s racially segregated railway cars violate the Constitution?

Connect: Jim Crow; equal protection

The Court upheld segregation under the separate-but-equal doctrine. Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented. Legal claims of equality did not make segregated systems equal in practice.
United States v. Wong Kim Ark
1898

Read opinion ↗

Was a U.S.-born child of Chinese subjects a citizen under the 14th Amendment?

Connect: Birthright citizenship; exclusion

The Court affirmed citizenship for Wong, born in San Francisco to Chinese parents permanently domiciled in the United States who were not diplomats. Distinguish citizenship from permission to immigrate.
Three comparisons to master: Munn ↔ Wabash (what can a state regulate?); Slaughterhouse ↔ Civil Rights Cases (which federal protections apply?); Plessy ↔ Wong Kim Ark (segregation and citizenship are distinct constitutional questions).

From recall to explanation

Essay lab

These are original practice prompts, not released UIL questions or predictions. Write an explanatory thesis, support it with precise evidence, and explain causes, consequences, or change over time.

Make a claim

Answer the question directly and identify the main relationship you will explain.

Prove it

Use named people, dated events, documents, or cases. Explain why each example supports your claim.

Add complexity

Compare perspectives, identify limits, or explain why a policy’s promise differed from its results.

The UIL rubric rewards a developed thesis, substantial relevant evidence, analysis, organization, and clear writing. A list of facts alone is insufficient.

1. Freedom and power

Explain why emancipation and constitutional change did not ensure full freedom for African Americans between 1865 and 1901. Address political, economic, and social forces.

R02 R03 R04 R06

Planning cues

Use an amendment, a labor arrangement, a form of violence, and an example of Black resistance. Explain the mechanism linking each to freedom.

2. Capitalism and government

Analyze how government action helped create the industrial economy and how reformers sought to regulate its power.

E01 E02 P02

Planning cues

Pair public land or railroad support with Munn, Wabash, the ICC, or Sherman. Avoid assuming government was absent from a supposedly laissez-faire economy.

3. Labor in comparative perspective

Compare the causes, methods, government responses, and outcomes of two major labor conflicts in the period.

L02 L03 L04 L05

Planning cues

Name the year, industry, organization, employer, and public authority. Distinguish the Haymarket rally from a strike.

4. A crisis of prosperity

Explain why industrial growth could coexist with economic insecurity, and compare two proposed responses to inequality.

E02 E05 S05 P03

Planning cues

Use a specific economic mechanism and compare the diagnoses and remedies of Carnegie, George, Sumner, or the Populists.

5. Who belongs?

Analyze tensions between expanding citizenship rights and exclusionary policies toward racial or ethnic groups.

R02 R06 S02 W02

Planning cues

Distinguish civil rights, voting rights, birthright citizenship, naturalization, immigration admission, and tribal sovereignty.

6. Farmers and the state

Explain the rise of agrarian protest and evaluate how Populist proposals addressed farmers’ economic problems.

E01 E06 P03 P04

Planning cues

Connect debt, crop prices, transport costs, and access to credit to specific demands. Compare Cleveland’s veto with the Omaha Platform.

7. Urban growth and reform

Explain how urbanization changed daily life and generated competing responses to social problems.

S01 S03 S04 P01

Planning cues

Compare machines, settlement houses, journalism, and women’s activism. Identify both the services they provided and their limits.

8. Expansion and national identity

Analyze how western expansion shaped economic development and competing ideas about national progress.

E01 W01 W02 W03

Planning cues

Compare Turner’s interpretation with evidence about railroads, settlement, land policy, and Indigenous experiences. Distinguish an author’s argument from your own analysis.
